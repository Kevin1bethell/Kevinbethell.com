<header class="page-head"><p class="eyebrow">Research</p><h1>Aesthetics, embodiment, and artistic practice.</h1></header>
<section class="section"><p class="eyebrow">Current work</p><div class="prose"><h2>Tattooing, aesthetics, and asceticism</h2><p>My work primarily explores aesthetic and ethical questions surrounding body art, particularly tattooing. My dissertation examines tattooing as a practice situated at the intersection of aesthetics and asceticism. Across cultures, ascetic traditions have employed bodily pain, discipline, and ritual in the pursuit of transformation, transcendence, or knowledge. Tattooing, meanwhile, is a contemporary artistic practice in which pain and aesthetic value are closely intertwined.</p><p>By placing tattooing in conversation with the history of ascetic practices, I investigate whether pain is merely a necessary condition of producing a lasting image or whether the experience of undergoing tattooing can itself contribute to the work’s aesthetic meaning. This inquiry also raises questions about the willing subject: how the decision to endure pain, and the transformation of the body through that decision, might shape the relationship between artistic creation and lived experience. Rather than assuming that tattooing is an ascetic practice, I aim to clarify both the affinities and the differences between these traditions, and what those comparisons reveal about embodiment, ritual, and aesthetic transformation.</p></div></section>
<section class="section"><p class="eyebrow">Recently finished projects</p><div class="prose"><h2>The Triadic Hermeneutics of Body Art: Expanding Gadamer’s Aesthetic Framework</h2><p>Body art—tattooing, scarification, and other forms of permanent bodily modification—poses a unique challenge to Hans-Georg Gadamer’s aesthetic framework. In <em>Truth and Method</em> and <em>The Relevance of the Beautiful</em>, Gadamer argues that the meaning of art emerges through a dialogical “fusion of horizons” enacted in the play, symbolism, and festival of aesthetic experience. Traditional artworks—paintings, sculptures, theatrical productions—fit this model in part because they exist as distinct objects that can be repeatedly encountered by shifting audiences across time. Body art, however, is never an object apart from its interpreter. It binds the artwork to a living, changing subject whose pain, permanence, and ongoing self-interpretation fundamentally alter the hermeneutic structure. While scholars such as Baltzer-Jaray and Rodriguez have begun to consider Gadamer in relation to body art, the theoretical implications of this medium have not been fully explored. This paper argues that body art reshapes Gadamer’s model by introducing a new hermeneutic role—the wearer—whose continuous, embodied engagement with the work transforms the aesthetic event into a triadic structure irreducible to traditional categories of artist and spectator.</p>
<h2>Agamben’s Dialectical Split Reconciled in Body Art</h2><p>This paper examines Giorgio Agamben’s diagnosis of a constitutive split at the heart of modern aesthetics—what he describes as a division between artistic creation and aesthetic judgment, genius and taste, artist and spectator. Drawing on Agamben’s reading of Hegel and Kant in <em>The Man Without Content</em>, the paper investigates body art as a site where this aesthetic division is destabilized. By binding artistic production to a living, feeling body, body art introduces a “lived-spectator” whose embodied experience complicates the opposition between creation and reception. Pain, endurance, and permanence disrupt disinterested judgment and reintroduce content into aesthetic experience. While body art does not fully overcome Agamben’s aesthetic split—remaining vulnerable to abstraction and commodification—it nevertheless reconfigures the relations between artist, material, and spectator. In doing so, it offers a partial reconciliation of poiesis and praxis, grounding aesthetic meaning in lived, embodied experience and challenging the dominance of detached spectatorship in modern aesthetics.</p>
<h2>Material Constraints and the Formation of the Japanese Tattoo Style</h2><p>This paper examines the historical formation of Japanese tattooing by considering how material limitations and inherited artistic practices shaped its distinctive visual style. Rather than concentrating on the symbolism of motifs such as dragons, koi, and tigers, it focuses on formal conventions: restricted color palettes, bold outlines, limited shading, and large-scale compositional clarity. Drawing on the history of ukiyo-e woodblock printing and Japanese tattooing, the paper argues that pigment availability, tattooing tools, healing knowledge, and the biological properties of skin converged with the formal vocabulary of print culture to shape the possibilities of tattoo production.</p><p>These constraints did more than restrict artistic choices: they generated recognizable aesthetic conventions. Through repetition, transmission, and the authority of tradition, solutions initially developed in response to material conditions became enduring stylistic norms. The persistence of these features despite later technological advances suggests that Japanese tattooing preserves the visual legacy of earlier practical limitations, demonstrating how material constraints can become constitutive of artistic style.</p>
<h2>A Proto-Spinozan Critique of Cartesian Dualism in 17th-Century Dutch Art</h2><p>This paper explores the intersection of philosophy, religion, and art in the seventeenth-century Dutch Republic, arguing that a proto-Spinozan critique of Cartesian dualism emerges not only in the philosophical writings of the period but also within artistic transformations accompanying the Protestant Reformation.</p><p>Placing Spinoza's metaphysics alongside the evolution of Dutch painting, the paper treats the two developments as parallel products of a shared intellectual and cultural milieu rather than claiming direct causal influence. Where Cartesian dualism separates mind and body, the project asks how Dutch art and Spinoza each reveal a growing emphasis on the immanence of mind, body, nature, and world.</p>
<h2>A Feminist Defense of Canonical Revision</h2><p>This paper offers a feminist defense of canonical revision in art history through the case of Artemisia Gentileschi. It considers both the systemic exclusion that shaped Gentileschi's reception and broader debates about aesthetic value, cultural significance, and the canon.</p><p>Drawing on Clifford Geertz, Gianni Vattimo, Steven Feierman, and Karen Halttunen, the paper argues that contextual interpretation can deepen rather than diminish aesthetic judgment. Canon formation is therefore treated not as apolitical recognition of self-evident greatness, but as an interpretive and philosophical practice requiring continued critique.</p>
<h2>Virum Post Machina: AI-Generated Art Considered in a Kantian Framework</h2><p>Could a Kantian conception of art allow AI-generated works to count as art? AI tools appear to lack qualities central to Kant's account—freedom, subjective experience, and purposiveness. This paper argues that the human prompt-imputer can instead serve as the source of these qualities.</p><p>By comparing AI-assisted creation with artists who intentionally employ external physical processes, the paper argues that freedom, subjective experience, and purposiveness can remain rooted in the human artist who establishes parameters, initiates the process, and directs it toward an artistic end.</p>
<h2>On Reason and Passion: A Reconciliation of Plato and Hume's Philosophies</h2><p>This project examines the apparent opposition between Plato's suspicion of passions that disrupt rational and moral order and Hume's claim that reason is subordinate to passion. Rather than treating the two as a simple dichotomy, the paper considers how their positions emerge from different philosophical frameworks and how each assigns an important role to both motivation and rational guidance.</p>
<h2>Kant's Aesthetics: Tattoos and Fine Art</h2><p>My master's thesis explains Kant's theories of art and fine art and argues that tattoos can be considered fine art within his aesthetic framework. After establishing how individual tattoos can qualify as works of art, the thesis turns to the larger question of what it means for an entire discipline to be a fine art.</p><p>I develop two accounts: the <em>Inherence View</em>, according to which a discipline inherently lends itself to producing fine art, and the <em>Cultural Entrenchment View</em>, according to which a discipline is culturally established around the intention to produce works of fine art. I argue that both are compatible with Kant's aesthetics and support treating tattooing as a fine-art discipline.</p></div></section>
