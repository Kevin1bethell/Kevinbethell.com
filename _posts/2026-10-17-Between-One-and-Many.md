I had the pleasure of attending Fatima Taylor’s solo exhibition, titled *Between One and Many*, last night at the Priscilla R. Tyson Cultural Arts Center in Columbus, Ohio. Fatima (whom I know personally and thus will refer to by her first name) puts her technical skill with the brush on full display in her portraits. However, while she shows no shortage of technical proficiency, the strength of her work lies in the *idea*. Upon first glance at each work, it is clear that it holds an idea inaccessible to those who rush. I had the opportunity to sit in front of each work for some time, many of which left me frustrated at my own inadequacies of artistic contemplation. Like Tantalus reaching for an apple, each time I reached for this *idea*, so evident yet unexplained, it receded, pulling me further into the work.

Of note, I found myself drawn to a few works in particular.

*Singular Gravity* displays a posed man standing in a dinghy amid a calm ocean, extending toward and harmoniously colliding with the skyline at the vertical halfway point of the composition. The man at once possesses immeasurable freedom, but is no less trapped. Able to go in any direction, yet none will lead him anywhere. Not resigned to a nihilistic slumber, he wills to stand; and in doing so, he is triumphant. As Nietzsche concludes his *Genealogy of Morality*: “man would much rather will *nothingness* than *not* will…” Perhaps, in his poised standing, the man has triumphed over nihilism. Perhaps he exemplifies it.

*Last Supper* displays a vivacious long table packed with men raising their cups. I am not an art historian, and so I will keep my descriptions of the work short and inadequate. This, similar to *Singular Gravity*, struck me as a bizarre display of freedom. The men sit at a table, their needs seemingly all met. They are both conquerors and conquered. They grip their cups with force, symptomatic of the very fragility of the system atop which they sit. What struck me about this painting was the apparent delight with which the men embrace their own captivity. They do not seem to experience their confinement as a restriction upon their freedom, but rather as an *expression of it*. I am reminded of Slavoj Žižek's observation in *Welcome to the Desert of the Real!* that we "feel free because we lack the very language to articulate our unfreedom." Perhaps what is most unsettling about these figures is not their captivity itself, but their apparent inability to recognize it as such.

*Fractured Horizons* displays a few dozen human figures buried up to their necks in the ground. Fellow attendees of the exhibition, however, read the work as depicting them up to their necks in water. While their interpretations may be more in line with what Fatima intended, my impressions of the work did not take place in this reality. Rather, my mind occupied a world in which the figures were buried in the ground. The bottom two-thirds of the composition shows their bodies being transformed into intertwining roots. My eye, however, remained upon the faces of the figures, some of which wailed in pain or agony, while others resigned themselves to their fates. One, however, just left of the middle of the picture frame, maintains an expression of peace. Perhaps this is his expression of freedom. His body, like the others, is trapped and morphing into the collective. But he is free. He has made peace with his fate, and in doing so, his freedom has transcended the captivity of his body.

All being said, I am very grateful to have had this opportunity. Fatima's work offered me much to contemplate, and I suspect that the ideas I found myself reaching for will continue to elude me for some time. Perhaps that is part of what makes the experience so worthwhile.
