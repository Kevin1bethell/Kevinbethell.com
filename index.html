Philosophical interests

Aesthetics, embodiment, and the history of philosophy.

My primary interests are in aesthetics and philosophy of art, especially questions surrounding body art, tattooing, embodiment, artistic practice, and aesthetic experience. My interdisciplinary work also engages substantially with art history, particularly where the historical development, material conditions, and visual conventions of artistic practices illuminate philosophical questions. I am particularly interested in cases that complicate familiar distinctions between artwork and experience, artist and spectator, aesthetic value and bodily transformation.

Much of my work develops through sustained engagement with Kant, Nietzsche, and German Idealism, as well as later continental thinkers who inherit and transform aspects of these traditions, particularly Gadamer and Agamben. I am interested both in the history of aesthetic theory and in what these traditions can contribute to contemporary philosophical problems.

My broader philosophical interests include ethics, the relationship between aesthetics and ethics, asceticism, philosophy of religion, interpretation and hermeneutics, and questions of artistic agency and authorship—including those raised by AI-generated art.

The Triadic Hermeneutics of Body Art: Expanding Gadamer’s Aesthetic Framework

Body art—tattooing, scarification, and other forms of permanent bodily modification—poses a unique challenge to Hans-Georg Gadamer’s aesthetic framework. Gadamer argues that the meaning of art emerges through a dialogical “fusion of horizons,” but body art is never an object apart from its interpreter. This paper argues that body art introduces a new hermeneutic role—the wearer—whose continuous, embodied engagement transforms the aesthetic event into a triadic structure...

Agamben’s Dialectical Split Reconciled in Body Art

This paper examines Giorgio Agamben’s diagnosis of a constitutive split at the heart of modern aesthetics: artistic creation and aesthetic judgment, genius and taste, artist and spectator. By binding artistic production to a living, feeling body, body art introduces a “lived-spectator” whose embodied experience complicates the opposition between creation and reception. Pain, endurance, and permanence disrupt disinterested judgment and reconfigure the relations between artist, material, and spectator...

Material Constraints and the Formation of the Japanese Tattoo Style

This paper examines the historical formation of Japanese tattooing through the relationship between material constraint and artistic style. Rather than focusing primarily on iconography or symbolic meaning, it argues that defining features of Japanese tattooing—including restricted palettes, bold outlines, and large-scale compositional unity—emerged through the convergence of pigment availability, tattooing technology, the biological demands of skin, and formal conventions inherited from ukiyo-e print culture. These practical solutions ultimately became durable aesthetic norms that persisted even after the constraints that produced them were technologically alleviated...

A Proto-Spinozan Critique of Cartesian Dualism in 17th-Century Dutch Art

This paper explores the intersection of philosophy, religion, and art in the seventeenth-century Dutch Republic, arguing that a proto-Spinozan critique of Cartesian dualism emerges not only in the philosophical writings of the period but also within artistic transformations accompanying the Protestant Reformation. Placing Spinoza’s metaphysics alongside the evolution of Dutch painting, the paper treats the two developments as parallel products of a shared intellectual and cultural milieu...

A Feminist Defense of Canonical Revision

This paper offers a feminist defense of canonical revision in art history through the case of Artemisia Gentileschi. It considers both the systemic exclusion that shaped Gentileschi’s reception and broader debates about aesthetic value, cultural significance, and the canon...

Could a Kantian conception of art allow AI-generated works to count as art? AI tools appear to lack qualities central to Kant’s account—freedom, subjective experience, and purposiveness. This paper argues that the human prompt-imputer can instead serve as the source of these qualities...

My master’s thesis explains Kant’s theories of art and fine art and argues that tattoos can be considered fine art within his aesthetic framework.
